---
title: Hamiltonian Simulation
description: How quantum computers simulate time evolution under a Hamiltonian.
---

Hamiltonian simulation asks a clean question: given a Hamiltonian $H$, how can a quantum computer approximate the evolution generated by that Hamiltonian? In the simplest time-independent case, the target unitary is

$$
U(t) = e^{-iHt},
\qquad
\lvert \psi(t) \rangle = U(t)\lvert \psi(0) \rangle.
$$

This is one of the most natural use cases for quantum computers because many systems in physics and chemistry are already described in exactly this language.[^ibm-sim][^lloyd][^georgescu]

IBM's "Simulating nature" lesson gives a good practical summary of the workflow: identify the system Hamiltonian, encode it, prepare an initial state, implement time evolution, optimize the circuit, execute it on hardware, and then post-process the results.[^ibm-sim-nature]

## Why this is useful

Classically simulating a generic quantum many-body system becomes hard because the state space grows exponentially with the number of qubits or particles. Quantum hardware can represent and manipulate these states more directly, which is why simulation has long been viewed as a leading quantum application area.[^lloyd][^georgescu]

Typical goals include:

1. Studying **real-time dynamics**.
2. Estimating **observables** such as magnetization or correlation functions.
3. Investigating **molecular and materials Hamiltonians**.
4. Approximating **ground or low-energy states**, often through related variational methods such as VQE.[^ibm-sim][^ibm-vqe-intro]

## From a Hamiltonian to a circuit

IBM's simulation lesson writes the Hamiltonian as a weighted sum of Pauli strings,

$$
H = \sum_j a_j P_j,
$$

where each $P_j$ is a tensor product of Pauli operators.[^ibm-sim] This is a practical representation because many quantum software tools can compile exponentials of Pauli strings into circuits. IBM's mapping lesson makes the same point more generally: once a problem has been rewritten into the right Hamiltonian or Pauli-operator form, the hard conceptual part of the mapping stage is largely done.[^ibm-mapping]

If all the terms commute, life is simple:

$$
e^{-i(H_1 + H_2)t} = e^{-iH_1 t} e^{-iH_2 t}.
$$

But the interesting cases are usually noncommuting, so we cannot factor the exponential exactly in that way.[^ibm-sim]

## Trotterization

The standard NISQ-friendly idea is to break time evolution into many small steps. For a Hamiltonian with Pauli terms,

$$
e^{-iHt}
\approx
\left(\prod_j e^{-i a_j P_j t/r}\right)^r,
$$

where $r$ is the number of time slices.[^ibm-sim]

This is often called **Trotterization** or a **product-formula** method. The key tradeoff is:

1. More time slices usually improve the approximation.
2. More time slices also make the circuit deeper.
3. Deeper circuits accumulate more hardware noise on NISQ machines.[^ibm-sim]

That tradeoff is one of the defining tensions of NISQ simulation.

## Example: the transverse-field Ising model

IBM's course uses the one-dimensional transverse-field Ising model as a concrete example:

$$
H
=
-J \sum_{i=0}^{N-2} Z_i Z_{i+1}
-h \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} X_i,
$$

with a slightly more general form including a field angle $\alpha$.[^ibm-sim]

This model is useful because it is simple enough to write down, but rich enough to study dynamics such as changes in magnetization and correlations over time. In software, IBM represents this Hamiltonian as a `SparsePauliOp`, then synthesizes a circuit for the corresponding evolution.[^ibm-sim]

## Hamiltonian simulation and quantum algorithms

It is accurate to say there is a close correspondence here, but it is worth stating it carefully.

Hamiltonian simulation is **already a quantum algorithm**: the algorithm takes a description of $H$ and $t$, and outputs a circuit approximating $e^{-iHt}$. At the same time, Hamiltonian evolution is a primitive used inside broader algorithms for phase estimation, chemistry, linear-systems-style methods, and other simulation-oriented workflows.[^ibm-sim][^lloyd]

So the clean summary is:

$$
\text{Hamiltonian simulation}
\;=\;
\text{both an application and an algorithmic primitive.}
$$

## Where NISQ helps and where it struggles

On NISQ hardware, Hamiltonian simulation is appealing because it maps naturally onto quantum devices. But it also exposes their weaknesses:

1. Long simulation times require deeper circuits.
2. Noncommuting Hamiltonians require approximation schemes.
3. Two-qubit gates and idle periods accumulate noise.
4. Results often need mitigation to be scientifically useful.[^ibm-sim][^ibm-mitigation]

That is why Hamiltonian simulation and error mitigation are tightly linked in practice. Better compilation, shallower decompositions, and mitigation methods are often as important as the abstract algorithm itself.

## Relation to variational simulation

Not every simulation task has to be done by direct real-time evolution. Variational approaches such as VQE attack related problems by optimizing a parameterized state rather than compiling a long exact evolution. In that sense:

1. **Direct Hamiltonian simulation** is usually about implementing $e^{-iHt}$ or closely related evolution.
2. **Variational simulation and VQE-like methods** use optimization to approximate properties of $H$, especially low-energy structure.[^ibm-vqa][^ibm-vqe-intro]

Both belong naturally in the NISQ toolkit.
